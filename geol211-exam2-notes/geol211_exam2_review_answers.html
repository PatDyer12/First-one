ATMOSPHERIC CIRCULATION

1Explain how/why air density changes.

  • Air density is controlled by temperature and moisture content.
  • Warm air: molecules spread out → less dense → rises. Cold air: molecules packed together → denser → sinks.
  • Water vapor (H₂O) is lighter than the N₂ (78% of air) and O₂ (21%) it replaces → humid air is LESS dense than dry air.
  • Densest air on Earth = the poles (cold + dry).

2Explain why regions of high/low density develop. Identify areas of high/low pressure. Describe the weather with each.

  • The Sun heats Earth unevenly (differential heating): the equator gets direct sunlight and is hot; the poles get spread-out sunlight and are cold.
  • Warm air rises → LOW pressure: rising air cools, water vapor condenses → clouds, rain, storms.
  • Cold air sinks → HIGH pressure: sinking air warms → clear, dry weather.
  • Air flows from high → low pressure = wind.
  • Where: LOW at 0° and 60° · HIGH at 30° and 90°.

3Draw the global atmospheric circulation pattern. Label high/low pressure, the ITCZ, and high/low precipitation.

  • 3 cells per hemisphere: Hadley (0–30°), Ferrel (30–60°), Polar (60–90°).
  • 0° = LOW = ITCZ (trade winds meet, air rises) → heavy rain. 30° = HIGH → dry, deserts. 60° = LOW → wet. 90° = HIGH → cold, dry.
  • Surface winds: trade winds (0–30°), westerlies (30–60°), polar easterlies (60–90°).

4Explain why the Coriolis Force exists and what the result is.

  • Why: Earth rotates. The equator travels farther each day (~40,000 km) than higher latitudes (~20,000 km at 60°), so it moves faster. A moving object keeps its original speed, so its path appears to curve.
  • Result: objects are deflected to the RIGHT in the Northern Hemisphere and to the LEFT in the Southern Hemisphere.
  • No Coriolis at the equator; it increases with latitude, speed and mass; it only matters for large motions (tens of km or more).
  • It does not cause wind — it changes the wind's direction.

5Explain the development of monsoons.

  • Land heats up and cools down faster than water.
  • Summer: land is hotter → air rises over land (LOW) → moist ocean air flows onto land → heavy rain (wet season).
  • Winter: land is colder → air sinks over land (HIGH) → dry air flows from land to sea → dry season.
  • Monsoons follow the ITCZ: June–July it's north over India (rain there); Dec–Jan it's south (rain in Darwin, Australia).

6Explain the development of land/sea breeze.

  • Same cause as monsoons (land heats/cools faster than water), but on a daily cycle.
  • Day: land warmer → air rises over land → cool air flows from sea to land = SEA BREEZE.
  • Night: land cooler → air sinks over land → air flows from land to sea = LAND BREEZE.

7List and describe the conditions necessary for a major hurricane to develop.

  • Warm water (>27°C) — supplies the heat and moisture (fuel).
  • Low-pressure system — air flows in and rises.
  • Warm air with high water vapor — when vapor condenses it releases latent heat, which powers the storm.
  • Low wind shear (<20 knots) — keeps the storm upright; strong shear tears it apart.
  • Strong Coriolis — makes it spin (cyclonic flow: counterclockwise in NH), so no hurricanes at the equator.

8List and describe the risks associated with hurricanes.

  • Hazards: waves, wind, rain, flooding, landslides, storm surge. Rain flooding can reach far inland (Ivan → Asheville NC, 2004).
  • Storm surge is caused by strong onshore winds pushing water onto the coast; worst at high tide (Sandy 2012).
  • Surge is higher with: stronger wind speed · bigger storm radius · forward speed (fast = higher surge, slow = longer flooding) · perpendicular (head-on) approach · wide, gently sloping continental shelf · coastline shape that funnels water.

9Predict the path of a hurricane based on the location of high/low pressure systems.

  • Air flows from high to low, so hurricanes are pushed around HIGH pressure (clockwise around a NH high) and pulled toward LOW pressure.
  • Atlantic: trade winds push it west → it curves north around the west side of the Bermuda High → westerlies push it northeast.
  • High extends far west → storm keeps going west (Florida/Gulf). High farther east → storm turns north sooner (East Coast or out to sea).

10Explain expected changes in hurricane activity due to climate change and trends observed over the last 100 years.

  • Expected: warmer water = more fuel → stronger, wetter storms; higher sea level → higher storm surge. Not necessarily more storms.
  • Last 100 years: no clear trend in the number of hurricanes (older records missed storms), but a larger share of strong (Cat 3–5) storms, more rain, and more damage as coasts are built up.

  • Salinity ≈ 35 ppt; highest in the subtropics (23.5–35°, evaporation) and lowest near rivers and rainy areas.

12Recognize the major oceanic currents.

  • Currents form gyres: clockwise in the NH, counterclockwise in the SH. The west side of each gyre is warm; the east side is cold.

13Describe the forces causing surface and deep currents.

  • Ocean circulation is controlled by wind, density, the position of the continents, and Coriolis.
  • Surface currents = driven by WIND (10% of ocean, fast). Coriolis turns them: Ekman transport moves water 90° to the right of the wind in the NH (left in SH).
  • Deep currents (thermohaline) = driven by DENSITY (temperature + salinity) and gravity (90% of ocean, slow).
  • Density is set at the surface: temperature (cooling by the air) and salinity (evaporation or sea-ice formation raise it; rain lowers it). Cold + salty water sinks.

14Explain why the speed of surface currents varies (eastern/western boundary currents).

  • Western boundary currents (Gulf Stream) are fast, narrow, deep, warm. Eastern boundary currents (Canary) are slow, wide, shallow, cold.
  • Why: the eastward rotation of Earth and Coriolis increasing with distance from the equator compress the western boundary current, so its speed increases.

15Explain how surface currents affect weather.

  • Currents transport heat from the equator toward the poles.
  • Warm currents warm the air → milder, wetter climates. Ex: Gulf Stream keeps Europe warm — January avg: Newfoundland 30/16°F vs Ireland 46/37°F (same latitude).
  • Cold currents cool the air → cooler, drier coasts.

16Explain how deep and surface water mix (upwelling/downwelling). Implications for marine life and climate?

  • Upwelling = deep, cold, nutrient-rich water rises to the surface. Downwelling = surface water sinks.
  • Coastal: wind along the coast + Ekman transport pushes surface water offshore → deep water rises (upwelling); pushed onshore → water piles up and sinks (downwelling).
  • Equatorial upwelling (water moves apart at the equator) and Southern Ocean upwelling (around Antarctica).
  • Marine life: upwelling brings nutrients to the sunlit surface → phytoplankton → rich fisheries. Downwelling carries oxygen down to deep water.
  • Climate: mixing moves heat and gases (O₂, CO₂) between the surface and deep ocean; upwelled cold water cools the coast.

17Recognize and explain the causes/effects of El Niño/La Niña. How does this affect hurricane activity?

  • Normal: trade winds push warm water west → warm and rainy in Indonesia/Australia; upwelling of cold water off Peru.
  • El Niño: trade winds weaken → warm water moves east → upwelling off Peru stops (fish decline); rain/floods in Peru, drought in Australia.
  • La Niña: trade winds strengthen → even more warm water west, colder east Pacific, stronger upwelling.
  • Hurricanes: El Niño → fewer Atlantic hurricanes (more wind shear), more Pacific. La Niña → more Atlantic hurricanes (less shear), fewer Pacific.
  • Anomaly = difference from the average (how SST maps show El Niño/La Niña).

18Explain how deep water circulation affects climate.

  • Deep water forms near the poles where water is cold and salty (sea ice leaves salt behind) → dense → sinks: North Atlantic Deep Water (near Greenland) and Antarctic Bottom Water.
  • This drives the conveyor belt: warm surface water flows to the North Atlantic, sinks, travels through the deep Atlantic, Indian and Pacific, rises, and returns.
  • It transports heat toward the poles (keeps Europe mild) and stores gases (CO₂, O₂) in the deep ocean.
  • If it slows (fresh meltwater makes water less dense so it can't sink) → abrupt cooling. Ex: Younger Dryas — Lake Agassiz meltwater → near-glacial cold; it ended with Greenland warming 10°C in a decade. The Atlantic overturning slowed in the 20th century.
