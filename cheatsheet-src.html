1. CAUSALITY — does X actually cause Y?

Individual effect: $\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$

ATE = average of $\tau_i$ over everyone.  ATT = average of $\tau_i$ over treated people only.

Observed gap $=$ ATT $+$ Selection bias

Observed gap $= E[Y(1)\mid D{=}1] - E[Y(0)\mid D{=}0]$

Selection bias $= E[Y(0)\mid D{=}1] - E[Y(0)\mid D{=}0]$

were the treated people already different, even without treatment?

STEPS: potential-outcomes table (Practice Q1)
  1. New column: $\tau = Y(1) - Y(0)$ for every row.
  2. ATE = average $\tau$ of all rows.
  3. Observed gap = avg $Y(1)$ of treated (“Yes”) rows − avg $Y(0)$ of untreated (“No”) rows.
  4. Compare: observed gap ≠ ATE → selection bias is present.
  5. ATT = average $\tau$ of treated rows only.
  6. Selection = avg $Y(0)$ of treated − avg $Y(0)$ of untreated.
  7. Check: ATT + Selection = Observed gap.
  • Fundamental problem of causal inference: we only see ONE outcome per person; the other (the counterfactual) is missing.
  • Random assignment (RCT): groups alike on average → selection bias $=0$ → treatment mean − control mean estimates the ATE. More credible because no one chooses their group.
  • Correlation ≠ causation: reverse causality (Y causes X); a confounder drives both; chance.
  • Prediction ≠ policy: X can predict Y by tracking hidden factors; changing X by policy doesn't change them.
  • Deterministic: Y exact from X (°F = 1.8·°C + 32). Stochastic: $Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+u$ (random part $u$).

2. PROBABILITY & STATISTICS

STEPS: PMF table → probabilities, E, Var (Practice Q2)
  1. Valid PMF? every $P\ge 0$ AND they add to 1.
  2. CDF: running total $F(x)=P(X\le x)$ at each value. Between values it's flat (staircase).
  3. “At least / more than”: 1 − rule: $P(X\ge 2)=1-P(X\le 1)$.
  4. “Given”: $P(X{\ge}2\mid X{\ge}1)=\dfrac{P(X\ge2)}{P(X\ge1)}$ (top is inside bottom).
  5. Columns: x | P | x·P | x²·P → add each column.
  6. $E[X]$ = sum of x·P.  $E[X^2]$ = sum of x²·P (square x, THEN × P).
  7. $\text{Var}=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2$ → $\text{SD}=\sqrt{\text{Var}}$. Don't square $E[X^2]$ again.
  8. If $C=a+bX$ ($C=30+8X$): $E[C]=a+b\,E[X]$; $\text{Var}(C)=b^2\,\text{Var}(X)$; $\text{SD}(C)=|b|\,\text{SD}(X)$. Units: dollars, dollars², dollars.

1 − rule: “greater than,” “at least,” or “not” → 1 − (the other side). $P(X\ge 1)=1-P(0)$. $P(X>b)=1-F(b)$. $P(Z>z)=1-\Phi(z)$. $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$.

STEPS: 2×2 joint table (Practice Q3)
  1. Read off: $P(A)$ = its row/column total; $P(A\text{ and }B)$ = the inside cell.
  2. $P(A\mid B) = \dfrac{\text{inside cell}}{B\text{'s total}}$. Name the reference group (“among rainy commutes…”).
  3. Independence test: compute $P(A)\cdot P(B)$, compare to the inside cell. SAME → independent. DIFFERENT → NOT independent. Write: “$P(A)P(B) = \_\_ \neq \_\_ = P(A\cap B)$, so not independent.”
  4. 0/1 variables: $E[RL]$ = inside cell → $\text{Cov} = \text{cell} - P(R)P(L)$. + → they happen together. NOT proof of causation (confounders).
STEPS: list of numbers (Practice Q4a–b)
  1. Sort. Mean = sum ÷ n. Median = middle value. Mode = most common.
  2. Columns: x | x − x̄ | (x − x̄)² → add the last column.
  3. $s^2 = \dfrac{\sum (x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1}$ ← divide by $n-1$, not $n$. Then $s=\sqrt{s^2}$.
  4. New units (×c, e.g. min → sec, $c=60$): mean, median, SD $\times c$; variance $\times c^2$.

$\text{Cov}(X,Y) = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]$ (+ together, − opposite).  $\text{Corr} = \rho = \dfrac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}$, between −1 and 1, no units.

Corr = 0 only means no straight-line relationship. $Y=X^2$ with $X$ centered at 0: Corr = 0 but $Y$ depends completely on $X$.

3. THE NORMAL DISTRIBUTION

STEPS: normal probability (Practice Q4c)
  1. Read $\mu$ and $\sigma$. $N(20,\,5^2)$ → SD = 5 (2nd number is the VARIANCE).
  2. z for each endpoint: $z = \dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$ (# of SDs from mean).
  3. Pick: $P(X\le b)=\Phi(z_b)$;  $P(X> b)=1-\Phi(z_b)$;  between: $\Phi(z_b)-\Phi(z_a)$.
  4. Negative z: $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$. Look up $\Phi$ in the table.
  5. Central 95% interval ≈ $\mu \pm 2\sigma$ (68% = ±1σ, 99.7% = ±3σ).

4. ESTIMATORS — how good is a guess?

Estimator = the formula/rule (random). Estimate = the number you get from your data.

Bias $= E[\hat\theta] - \theta$. Unbiased = right on average.  Efficient: among unbiased, smaller variance.

Consistent: closer to the truth as $n$ grows (bias → 0 and variance → 0).  MSE $=\text{Var} + \text{Bias}^2$ (lower is better).

STEPS: compare estimators (Practice Q5, Q6)
  1. Estimates: count $S$ = # of 1s, $n$ = sample size, plug into each formula.
  2. E: write it as $(aS+b)/c$ → $E=(a\cdot np+b)/c$ (uses $E[S]=np$). For $k\bar X$: $E=k\mu$.
  3. Bias = E − true value ($p$ or $\mu$). Bias = 0 → unbiased.
  4. Var: $(aS+b)/c$ → $a^2\cdot np(1-p)/c^2$ (uses $\text{Var}(S)=np(1-p)$). For $k\bar X$: $k^2\sigma^2/n$.
  5. MSE = Var + Bias². Plug in numbers (e.g. $p=.52$, $n=11$) → smaller MSE wins.
  6. Consistent? Let $n\to\infty$: do bias AND Var → 0? ($n$ in denominator → yes; $X_1$ → no.)
  7. Efficient? Among the unbiased ones, pick the smaller Var.

5. REGRESSION (OLS)

Model: $Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + u$.  OLS = the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals.

$\hat\beta_1 = \dfrac{\sum (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum (x_i-\bar x)^2}$     $\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1\,\bar x$

Also $\hat\beta_1 = r\cdot\dfrac{s_Y}{s_X}$ → slope has the same sign as the correlation.

STEPS: OLS by hand from data (Practice Q7)
  1. Find $\bar x$ and $\bar y$.
  2. Columns: x | y | x−x̄ | y−ȳ | (x−x̄)² | (x−x̄)(y−ȳ)
  3. Add up the last two columns → $\hat\beta_1$ = (last column sum) ÷ ($(x-\bar x)^2$ column sum).
  4. Write $\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x$ with letters, THEN plug in numbers.
  5. Add 2 columns: $\hat y=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x$ and $\hat u=y-\hat y$. $\hat u>0$ → point is ABOVE the line.
  6. Check: residuals add to 0, and $\sum x\hat u = 0$ (multiply x × û, add).
  7. Check: plug $\bar x$ into your line, you must get $\bar y$. If not, the intercept is wrong.
  8. Predict: $\hat y=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x$ at the new x. Outside the data range → extrapolation, less reliable.
STEPS: OLS from given sums (Practice Q8c)
  1. $\hat\beta_1 = \sum(x-\bar x)(y-\bar y) \div \sum(x-\bar x)^2$ (e.g. 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5).
  2. $\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1\bar x$ (e.g. 12 − 1.5·4 = 6). Line: $\hat y = 6 + 1.5x$ → at x = 6, $\hat y = 15$.

Changing units (Q7d, Q8d, Q9g): multiply $y$ by $c$ → slope and intercept both $\times c$. Multiply $x$ by $c$ → slope $\div c$, intercept same. (Dollars → cents: slope $\times 100$.)

Why: top of $\hat\beta_1$ scales by $c_xc_y$, bottom by $c_x^2$; then $\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x$. Same fitted line, just new units.

STEPS: derive OLS (Practice Q8a–b)
  1. Minimize $Q=\sum (y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)^2$ (squared so + and − misses don't cancel).
  2. $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial b_0} = -2\sum(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0$ → $\sum \hat u_i = 0$
  3. $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial b_1} = -2\sum x_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0$ → $\sum x_i\hat u_i = 0$
  4. Divide step 2 by $n$: $\bar y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1\bar x$ → $\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x$; the line passes through $(\bar x,\bar y)$.

Slope: “Each additional [unit of X] is associated with a $\hat\beta_1$ [units of Y] change in Y, on average.”

Intercept: predicted Y when X = 0 (often meaningless if X = 0 is outside the data).

Not causal because: units with more X may differ in other ways (ability, income) that sit in $u$; no random assignment.

Key assumption: $E[u\mid X]=0$ → the “other factors” average to zero at every X → the line = average Y at each X. Not needed to calculate $\hat\beta$, only to interpret it. Fails if, e.g., income affects scores AND is related to class size.

Residuals adding to 0 is just math — it does not prove $E[u\mid X]=0$.

Reading R output (Q9f): $\widehat{\text{Score}} = 698.93 - 2.28\cdot\text{ClassSize}$. Degrees of freedom $= n-2$ → 418 df → $n = 420$. $R^2$ (.051) = share of variation in Y explained by the line (5.1%). Descriptive only — not causal.
